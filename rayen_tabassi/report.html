01 Motivation

Recipe recommendation platforms like MealMate succeed when they surface content that users genuinely enjoy. Understanding what attributes drive a recipe to be liked or saved — beyond simple star ratings — can help improve recommendation algorithms, guide content creators, and reveal what food preferences look like at scale.

This project tackles the question: can we predict whether a recipe will be liked based on measurable attributes like prep time, calorie content, ingredient count, and cuisine type? Rather than relying on user history, we focus on recipe-intrinsic features that are available before any user interaction occurs.

🎯 Research Question

Can we predict recipe popularity (liked vs. not liked) from nutritional and complexity features alone?

💡 Why It Matters

Cold-start recommendations — for recipes with no prior ratings — require feature-based models. This work directly addresses that problem.

02 Data Source

The primary dataset is the Food.com Recipes and Interactions dataset from Kaggle, containing over 180,000 recipes and 700,000 user interactions. A filtered and enriched subset of 500 recipes and ~7,000 interactions across ~150 users was used for this analysis.

A secondary dataset — the Kaggle Recipe Ingredients Dataset — was used to assign cuisine labels to each recipe, serving as the data enrichment required by the project guidelines.

04 Hypothesis Testing

Five hypothesis tests were conducted at significance level α = 0.05 to investigate specific claims about recipe popularity.

TestMethodp-valueDecision
T1: Quick (≤30 min) recipes get higher ratings Mann-Whitney U (one-sided) 0.574 Fail to Reject H₀
T2: More ingredients → lower rating Spearman Correlation 0.834 Fail to Reject H₀
T3: High-calorie recipes rated differently Mann-Whitney U (two-sided) 0.874 Fail to Reject H₀
T4: Liked status depends on cuisine type Chi-Square Independence 0.063 Fail to Reject H₀
T5: Ratings differ across cuisine groups Kruskal-Wallis H 0.078 Fail to Reject H₀

Interpretation: None of the five tests reached significance at α = 0.05. This is a meaningful finding in itself — it suggests that individual recipe attributes (prep time, calories, ingredient count, cuisine) do not independently drive rating differences in a statistically detectable way. Ratings on Food.com are consistently high across the board, likely reflecting a platform-wide positivity bias. Predicting popularity may require capturing feature interactions rather than single-variable effects.

T1 — Quick vs slow recipes: no significant difference.
T4 — Liked status vs cuisine: p=0.063, borderline.

Recipes cluster into four archetypes based on nutritional and complexity features: quick-and-light, hearty-and-complex, calorie-dense, and a general "other" group. These clusters align intuitively with real recipe categories.

06 Key Findings

📊

Ratings are uniformly high. The vast majority of recipes score 4–5 stars, suggesting a strong positivity bias on the platform. This compressed rating range makes prediction difficult and explains why no single feature drives significant rating differences.

🧪

No individual feature significantly predicts ratings. All five hypothesis tests failed to reject H₀ at α=0.05. Prep time, calorie content, ingredient count, and cuisine type — in isolation — do not reliably separate liked from not-liked recipes.

🤖

The Decision Tree outperforms other classifiers with 63% accuracy and F1=0.64, suggesting that combinations of features (captured by tree splits) carry more signal than any individual feature alone.

📉

Recipe attributes explain only 17% of rating variance. The Linear Regression R² of 0.169 confirms that what makes a recipe popular goes beyond nutrition and complexity — user context, social proof, and personal taste likely dominate.

🍳

Cuisine type shows the strongest (borderline) signal. Test 4 (Chi-Square, p=0.063) was the closest to significance, with Mexican recipes having a notably higher liked rate. This warrants further investigation with a larger sample.

🗂️

K-Means reveals four natural recipe archetypes — quick/light, hearty/complex, calorie-dense, and mixed — which could form the basis of a recipe type taxonomy for future recommendation work.

07 Limitations & Future Work

⚠️ Limitations

Small sample size. Only 500 recipes were used. A larger sample would increase statistical power and improve model generalizability.

Rating compression. Ratings cluster tightly between 4.5–5.0, making it very hard to distinguish liked from not-liked recipes using numeric features.

No text features. Recipe titles, instructions, and reviews were not used. Sentiment and linguistic complexity likely carry strong predictive signal.

Simulated saved_rate. The saved flag is a proxy derived from rating and review length, not actual bookmark data.

🚀 Future Work

NLP on reviews. Applying TF-IDF or BERT embeddings to review text could dramatically improve classification performance.

Collaborative filtering. Incorporating user-recipe interaction history would enable personalized recommendations beyond feature-based models.

Larger dataset. Running the full pipeline on all 180K+ recipes would provide more robust hypothesis test results and better ML models.

Deep learning. Recipe image analysis combined with ingredient features could capture visual appeal as a popularity driver.
